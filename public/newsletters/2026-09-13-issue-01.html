    Mechanics

    Six things worth actually understanding

    If you are new to the club, start here. Every one of these shows up in the stories above.

    TL;DRBond prices and yields move opposite ways, the curve tells you what traders expect from the Fed, and rate moves hit small companies before they hit mega-cap tech.

    Fixed income

    Price and yield move in opposite directions

    bond price ↓  ⇔  bond yield ↑

    A bond pays a fixed stream of dollars. If nobody wants it, its price falls, and since the dollars it pays are unchanged, whoever buys it at the lower price earns a higher return. That return is the yield.

    So "yields surged this week" and "the bond market sold off this week" are the same sentence. Every headline about rising yields is a headline about falling bond prices.

    Curve

    The 2s10s spread, and what flattening says

    2s10s = 10-year yield − 2-year yield

    Normally longer money costs more, so the spread is positive. It closed the week at 33 bp, down from 41 bp a week earlier.

    It flattened for a specific reason: the 2-year jumped 26 bp on rate-hike odds while the 30-year rose only 11 bp. Traders repriced the next two years of Fed policy without much changing their view of the next thirty.

    Inflation

    Headline vs. core CPI

    headline 3.4%  ·  core 2.4%

    Headline CPI counts everything. Core strips out food and energy, because those two swing on weather, wars and shipping lanes rather than on how hot the economy is.

    The Fed watches core to judge whether inflation is embedded. But it cannot ignore headline forever: households experience gas prices, and if high energy costs last long enough they leak into wages and into everything else.

    Policy tools

    What a Treasury buyback is supposed to do

    Treasury buys back bonds → fewer bonds outstanding → price ↑, yield ↓

    The Treasury repurchases its own outstanding debt. Less supply in the market should mean higher prices and therefore lower yields. That is the whole theory.

    It works on the margin. It does not work against the thing actually pushing yields up, which is the sheer volume of new debt being issued and doubt about inflation. That mismatch is this week's second story.

    Volatility

    Reading the VIX

    VIX 15.84  ·  long-run average ≈ 19–20

    The VIX is built from S&P 500 option prices and expresses the market's expected annualized volatility over the next 30 days. It is a price for insurance, not a forecast of direction.

    A close under 16 during a week when the bond market hit 19-year highs is the interesting detail: equity investors are not paying up for protection. Either they are relaxed, or they are complacent, and that is the argument.

    Sensitivity

    Why small caps take rate moves hardest

    Russell 2000 −2.4%  vs  S&P 500 −0.8%

    Small companies carry more floating-rate debt, refinance more often, and hold less cash. When the cost of borrowing jumps, their interest expense reprices almost immediately.

    Mega-cap tech mostly termed out its debt years ago at low fixed rates and sits on large cash balances that now earn more. Same rate move, opposite effect on the income statement.

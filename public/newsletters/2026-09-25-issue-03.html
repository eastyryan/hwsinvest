The recap

The week in one paragraph

The week belonged to the bond market's mood swing and the software aisle's AI repricing. The Nasdaq jumped 2.1% to 27,068.72, the S&P 500 added 1.2% to 7,743.41, and the Dow inched up 0.3% to 51,828.62, while the Russell 2000 fell 0.8% and the equal-weight S&P 500 slipped 0.2%, a narrow rally led by chips and AI agents. Meta's Muse assistant topped the App Store and added $192 billion in a single session, while Microsoft's Copilot "super app" and a record $11.6 billion Anthropic-Akamai cloud deal kept the AI trade alive. The hangover: the Fed's September 16 rate hike to 3.75–4.00% under new Chair Kevin Warsh, plus a 58.4 September flash PMI (the strongest since July 2021), pushed the 10-year Treasury to 5.17%, a 19-year high, and the 30-year to 5.49%. Oil whipsawed on Iran war headlines and Brent settled at $104.32. Bitcoin rose 3.9% to $84,056. What matters next: August PCE on Wednesday, the September jobs report on Friday, and whether CME odds of another October hike hold near 64%.

The week

Stories

Rates

A hawkish hike under a new chair, and a chorus promising more

The market is still digesting the September 16 FOMC decision: a unanimous 12-0 vote to raise the federal funds target range 25 basis points to 3.75–4.00%, the first hike since July 2023, with new Chair Kevin Warsh at the podium. Sixteen of eighteen FOMC participants signaled at least one more hike before year end, and officials now do not expect inflation back at target until 2029. Warsh's message was blunt: "our predominant focus is on the price-stability side of our mandate. The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long."

The officials who followed him this week sounded even more direct. Fed Governor Michael Barr said on Wednesday that "risks to achieving our inflation target have increased, while risks to the labor market have receded," and that "in my base case, further policy adjustments are likely to be needed." New York Fed President John Williams said on Thursday that "it's likely that another rate hike may be appropriate by the end of the year," while Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said "some modest further tightening may be warranted" and Cleveland's Beth Hammack warned that "the longer that high inflation persists, the more challenging and costly it can be to bring it back down."

The bond market heard them. The 10-year set a 19-year closing high of 5.18% on Thursday and finished Friday at 5.17%, the 30-year closed at 5.49% (its highest since 2004 on an intraday basis Thursday), and a weak $70 billion 5-year auction midweek added to the selloff. The 2s10s spread (the 10-year yield minus the 2-year yield) widened to 36 basis points from 25, meaning the long end sold off harder than the short end: investors demanded a bigger term premium (extra yield for holding long-term bonds) because they see inflation staying higher for longer, not just because the Fed is tight right now.

CME FedWatch put the odds of another hike at the October 27–28 meeting at 64.2% on Thursday, up from about 58% the week before, though the number bounced with the data all week. One voice was conspicuously quiet: Warsh himself, who refuses to give forward guidance. As Citi's chief U.S. economist Andrew Hollenhorst put it, stable labor data have "convinced most Fed officials that downside employment risks have been so diminished that focus can shift entirely toward inflation." For undergrads watching at home: the Fed just told you rates are going up again until inflation, not jobs, cooperates.

TL;DR The Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00% and nearly every speaker this week said more is coming. Bonds sold off hard, with the 10-year at a 19-year high. The market gives the October hike about a two-in-three chance.
Geopolitics

The Iran war's whiplash week

The U.S.-Israeli war with Iran has run since late February, and the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a third of the world's seaborne oil flows, remains the market's pressure point. Monday set the tone when UK Maritime Trade Operations reported a tanker entering the strait was struck by a projectile, injuring two crew members, with a second tanker hit by debris. Brent opened the week near $104.

Tuesday flipped the script. Iran offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the U.S. eased military pressure, U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff held "lengthy" indirect talks with the Iranian delegation in New York, and Saudi Aramco began loading more crude at Persian Gulf terminals. Brent fell 2.6% to $97.76 and WTI dropped 3.1% to $92.78. The mechanism is simple: a reopened strait means more tankers get through, more supply reaches the market, and the fear premium (the extra price traders pay for the risk of disruption) shrinks.

Wednesday brought the snapback. Iran's president told the UN General Assembly that Tehran "would never surrender to U.S. pressure," security chief Mohsen Rezaei said the strait would stay closed while Iran's conditions are unmet, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said a deal "would involve hard work over a period of time," adding the president kept military options open. Separately, Energy Secretary Chris Wright told oil executives to brace for possible U.S. diesel-export curbs after reports the White House was weighing a 90-day diesel export ban (which the White House denied), sending diesel futures down about 5% midday before the talk faded. Brent climbed roughly 4% on the day.

Thursday's Houthi missile and drone attacks on Aramco facilities in Yanbu and a target in Riyadh, plus a senior Iranian official threatening to widen the conflict to the Indian Ocean, pushed Brent 3.4% higher to settle at $106.60. Friday finally cooled on truce hopes, with Brent settling at $104.32 and WTI at $92.41. Kpler data showed Hormuz flows at 33.7 million barrels in the week starting September 20, roughly on track with the prior week, which is why the premium keeps draining out on every hopeful headline.

TL;DR Oil spent the week reacting to diplomacy, not fundamentals: tanker strikes and Houthi attacks on Tuesday and Thursday spiked prices, while truce talk and truce hopes kept pulling them back. Brent finished nearly flat on the week at $104.32.
Energy

Diesel, not crude, is where the war is biting

Look past Brent's round trip and the real energy story is diesel. U.S. diesel prices sit at record highs, up more than 80% year to date, and the talk of a 90-day U.S. diesel export ban on Wednesday showed how exposed the market is. An export ban would keep diesel at home and lower domestic prices in theory, which is why diesel futures fell about 5% midday on the headline. But Energy Secretary Wright told executives publicly that a ban "would not work," even as President Trump said he would support one. The contradiction is the point: Washington is reaching for tools it may not use, and traders are pricing the reach itself.

Refiners felt the squeeze in their share prices. Marathon Petroleum ($MPC) fell 7.4% and Valero ($VLO) fell 6.3% on the week, because a diesel export ban would hit refiner margins directly: if U.S. diesel cannot be sold abroad, domestic supply rises and the premium collapses. The energy sector as a whole fell 2.9%, one of the week's worst performers, even though crude stayed above $100. That disconnect is a reminder that oil companies do not just own oil; they own the difference between crude and the refined products they sell, and policy can compress that difference fast.

The inventory picture added noise. EIA data Wednesday showed crude inventories rising 3 million barrels to 426.4 million (analysts had expected a 641,000-barrel draw), while distillates fell 428,000 barrels to 107.4 million. On a normal week, a crude build would press prices down. This was not a normal week, so the build was ignored entirely.

Zoom out and the asymmetry is striking. Brent and WTI diverged: Brent finished the week up 0.4% at $104.32 while WTI fell 3.8% on a roll-adjusted basis. As Phillip Nova's Priyanka Sachdeva explained, Brent carries the Hormuz sea-route premium because it is priced off Middle East supply, while WTI benefits from relatively insulated U.S. production. Until the strait fully reopens, that gap is the trade.

TL;DR Diesel, not crude, is where the war is biting: record prices and export-ban talk crushed refiner stocks. Brent held its fear premium while WTI faded on truce hopes, and physical Hormuz flows stayed near normal the whole time.
Equities

The AI agent trade goes mainstream, and picks its victims

The week's equity story had a single theme: AI agents. Meta's Muse assistant topped Apple's App Store, and Meta ($META) jumped 12.9% on the week, adding $192 billion in market value on Monday alone in its best day since April 2025. UBS argued agents give Meta a third AI revenue stream through transaction share, and Shopify and PayPal integrated Muse into checkout, while Amazon blocked it from purchases. Friday's 3.3% drop to $751.66, after a New Mexico jury found Facebook liable for deceiving users in the Cambridge Analytica scandal, barely dented the week's gains.

Microsoft ($MSFT, +4.5%) launched a Copilot "super app" with an autonomous agent, and the chip trade followed. Astera Labs ($ALAB, +20%) expanded its Leo memory-controller lineup for agentic AI workloads, AMD ($AMD, +12.7%) closed above $1 trillion in market value for the first time with Meta as its second-largest customer, Intel ($INTC, +13.3%) rallied as CEO Lip-Bu Tan said the company can meet only about half of customer demand, and Qualcomm ($QCOM, +13.6%) reversed an initial selloff from its renewed Apple patent deal to finish strong.

But look under the index and the rally was narrow. Just 5 of 11 sectors advanced, and the equal-weight S&P 500 fell 0.2%. The flip side of the agent trade is the agent victim trade: Paychex ($PAYX, -13%) beat on earnings but warned its Management Solutions segment came in slightly below expectations, Intuit ($INTU, -11%) sits 55–60% below its July 2025 peak, and Charles Schwab ($SCHW, -5.9%) and Wells Fargo ($WFC, -3.7%) fell on fears that agents let customers move money between providers more easily. Even Airbnb ($ABNB, -5.3%) and Booking ($BKNG, -2.4%) sold off on AI travel disruption fears.

Nvidia ($NVDA, +1.3%) was the odd one out, essentially flat as investor doubt about its stand-alone CPU push and Treasury yield pressure offset its financing news. Oracle ($ORCL, -7.1%) had the worst week of the megacaps after Bloomberg reported a force majeure notice on its Project Jupiter data center in New Mexico, with its credit-risk gauge hitting a record high. The message: this market pays for AI revenue today and punishes AI promises that get more expensive tomorrow.

TL;DR AI agents drove everything: Meta and Microsoft surged on agent products, chips ripped, and the Nasdaq outperformed. But the same trade crushed software and financial incumbents, and the gains were concentrated in a handful of names.
Housing & credit

Housing and credit feel a 19-year-high 10-year

The 10-year's climb to a 19-year high landed fastest in housing. The Mortgage Bankers Association's 30-year fixed rate rose to 7.12%, a more-than-two-year high, while Freddie Mac's weekly measure hit 7.03%, its highest since January 2025 and up more than 100 basis points since the Iran war started. For a sophomore keeping score: the 10-year Treasury is the benchmark mortgage rates are priced off, so every extra tenth of a point in the 10-year makes home loans more expensive.

Against that backdrop, Thursday's new home sales number was a genuine surprise: 684,000 at a seasonally adjusted annual rate in August, up 6.4% from July and well above the 615,000 consensus, the highest since December 2025. Builders are discounting: the median new-home price fell 5.8% from a year ago to $393,700. So the story is not that demand is booming at 7% mortgages; it is that builders are cutting prices to move inventory, and it is working.

Corporate credit is showing the strain too. Oracle's credit-risk gauge hit a record high after the Project Jupiter force majeure report, and utilities were the week's worst sector (-3.1%) as regulators took a closer look at NextEra's planned $67 billion merger with Dominion ($NEE -5.5%, $D -4.6%). The AI buildout is being financed in the bond market: Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle have issued roughly $223 billion in bonds this year, more than 40% with maturities of 20 years or longer. Locking in 20-year money while the 30-year is at 5.49% is expensive, but it shows these companies expect the AI returns to cover it.

There is an irony in the data. Initial jobless claims fell to 197,000, near 57-year lows, and the September flash PMI hit 58.4, the strongest since July 2021, with input costs rising the fastest since October 2022. As FWDBONDS' Christopher Rupkey put it, "in mid-September the economy seems to be firing on all cylinders in part due to the extraordinary capex expenditures on AI." A strong economy is good news, unless you are the Fed, in which case it is the reason for another hike.

TL;DR The 5% 10-year pushed mortgage rates to multiyear highs, yet new home sales beat thanks to builder discounts. Corporate credit is straining at the edges, and the hyperscalers are locking in long-term bond money to fund the AI buildout anyway.

Mechanics

Six things worth actually understanding

If you are new to the club, start here. Every one of these shows up in the stories above.

Curve

Bear steepening, and why this week is not last week

2y +5 bp  ·  10y +16 bp  →  2s10s +11 bp

"Bear" means yields rose (bond prices fell). "Steepening" means the long end rose more than the short end, so the gap between them widened. That is what happened this week: the 2s10s went from 25 bp to 36 bp.

Last week was the opposite shape. The 2-year jumped on the hike while the 30-year barely moved, a policy repricing. This week the long end did the moving, which says investors are worried about inflation and debt years out, not just about the next Fed meeting.

Fixed income

Term premium: the part of the yield the Fed does not set

10-year yield ≈ expected path of short rates + term premium

If you lend for ten years instead of rolling one-year loans, you take on risk that inflation or supply surprises you in between. The term premium is the extra yield you demand for that.

The Fed controls the short rate. It does not control the term premium, and that is what moved this week. A hot PMI, hawkish speakers and heavy issuance all make lenders ask for more to go long.

Auctions

How a weak Treasury auction moves yields

weak demand → auction clears at a higher yield → the whole curve reprices

The Treasury sells new debt at auction, and buyers bid the yield they will accept. When demand is thin, the auction has to clear at a higher yield than the market was showing just before. Traders call that gap a tail.

A tail matters beyond the day because it tells everyone what the marginal buyer now needs. That is why a weak $70 billion 5-year auction midweek pushed the rest of the curve up with it.

Macro data

Reading a PMI: the 50 line

above 50 = expanding  ·  below 50 = contracting

A purchasing managers' index surveys companies on whether output, orders, hiring and prices went up or down this month. 50 is the dividing line. The flash reading is an early estimate built from most of the month's responses.

58.4 is a strong, broad expansion. For the Fed the sub-index that matters is input prices, which rose the most since October 2022. Strong growth plus rising costs is the combination that makes another hike more likely.

Energy

The crack spread: why refiners fell while oil held

3-2-1 crack ≈ (2 × gasoline + 1 × diesel − 3 × crude) ÷ 3

A refiner buys crude and sells gasoline and diesel. Its profit is the gap between the two, called the crack spread, not the price of oil itself.

That is why Marathon and Valero fell 6–7% in a week when Brent finished flat. A diesel export ban would trap diesel at home, push its price down and squeeze the spread, even if crude never moves.

Energy

Brent vs. WTI: two prices for one commodity

Brent $104.32 − WTI $92.41 ≈ $11.91 a barrel

Brent is priced off seaborne crude from the North Sea and competes directly with Middle East barrels. WTI is priced in Cushing, Oklahoma, off U.S. production that does not need to pass through Hormuz.

Normally the two sit a few dollars apart. When the risk is a shipping lane, Brent carries the fear premium and WTI does not, so the gap widens. Until the strait fully reopens, that spread is the cleanest read on how much war risk the market is pricing.

TL;DR When the long end rises faster than the short end, markets are pricing inflation and debt risk, not just the Fed. And in energy this week, the spreads between prices told you more than any single price.

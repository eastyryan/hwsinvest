The recap

The week in one paragraph

Stocks made records in a bond market that briefly looked like 2002, then gave the AI trade a scare and took most of it back. The Nasdaq closed at records on Monday and Tuesday, and the S&P 500 joined it Tuesday at 7,818.93, even with the 10-year Treasury at 5.31% on Monday, the highest close since April 2002. September payrolls, out the Friday before, had added only 29,000 jobs, and Wednesday's Fed minutes said most officials still expect another rate increase by year end. The indexes finished higher anyway: the S&P at 7,811.54, up 1.2%, the Dow at 51,654.95, up 0.9%, and the Nasdaq at 27,366.17, up 0.6%. Small caps did not come along. The Russell 2000 fell 0.9%. Under the surface the leadership flipped from September: utilities, staples and energy led, and technology was last. Thursday was the break. A Financial Times report on OpenAI's revenue knocked the semiconductor index 3.39% and the Nasdaq 1.25%, and Friday's rebound repaired the indexes more than the chip names. The same Friday, SpaceX's plan to buy spectrum for a Starlink mobile service repriced the carriers in a few hours. Brent's December contract settled at $104.72, up 2.4%. What matters next is September CPI on Wednesday, and Tuesday morning's bank earnings. The Fed meets again October 27–28.

The week

Stories

Rates

The 10-year printed a 24-year high, then gave the week back

The number that will get quoted is Monday's. The 10-year Treasury closed at 5.31% on the Treasury's own par yield curve, the highest close since April 2, 2002. That is above the 5.17% close this letter reported for September 25. The high in between was 5.29% on September 30. This week's close actually fell, from 5.28% on October 2 to 5.24% on October 9. The path was not a straight line. On Wednesday, after the minutes, the traded 10-year spiked as high as 5.36% and the par curve still closed the day at 5.28%.

The minutes are from the September 15–16 meeting, the one that raised the federal funds target to 3.75–4.00% under Chair Kevin Warsh, the first hike since 2023. The line that mattered: "Most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent tried to calm the early-week selloff, saying the rise in U.S. yields lined up with the rest of the world and did not warrant alarm. The auctions helped that calm stick. Demand was solid at Wednesday's 10-year sale and Thursday's 30-year sale, and yields drifted lower into the weekend.

Read the level and the odds separately. Heading into the weekend, rate markets had a December hike fully priced. A hike at the October 27–28 meeting is not the base case. ING economist James Knightley said it would take "an especially hot CPI report" on Wednesday to put an October hike back in play, and even then he expects the Fed to wait until December. The 2-year, the maturity that listens hardest to the next few meetings, fell 3 basis points to 4.80%. The 30-year fell 3 basis points to 5.60%. The 2s10s spread, the 10-year minus the 2-year, went from 45 basis points to 44. This was not a week the curve changed its mind. It was a week the 10-year printed a headline and then backed off it.

The places a 5% 10-year shows up in real life moved too. Freddie Mac's weekly survey put the 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.40%. Investment-grade corporate bonds yielded more than 6% for the first time since 2023. The New York Fed's September survey had one-year inflation expectations up to 3.9% from 3.6%, with the three-year up a tenth to 3.3% and the five-year steady at 3.0%. Households, homebuyers and bond investors are all looking at the same yield.

TL;DR The 10-year closed Monday at a high last seen in April 2002, then finished the week at 5.24%. The Fed's minutes still point to another hike by year end. December is priced. October is not, unless CPI surprises.
Equities

Records on Tuesday, a chip selloff on Thursday, a bounce on Friday

Monday the Nasdaq closed at a record, 27,477.31, up 1.05%, and the S&P rose 0.66% to 7,773.95. Tuesday the S&P joined it at 7,818.93, after trading as high as 7,844.52, and the Nasdaq made a second record at 27,599.79. It was the first S&P record close with the 10-year yield above 5% since July 19, 2007. A stock market at a high and a bond yield at a 24-year high is an unusual pair. It says investors will pay up for earnings even while the discount rate on those earnings is the highest in a generation.

Wednesday cracked the Dow, not the growth trade. The average fell 341 points, or 0.66%, and Caterpillar alone fell 5.75% to $813.83. The Dow weights its 30 stocks by share price, so an expensive share moves it more than a large company with a cheaper share. The S&P and the Nasdaq each fell only 0.22% that day. The Russell 2000 fell 1.31%, the worst session of the week for small caps.

Thursday was the AI scare. The Financial Times, citing documents shared with investors, reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue was about $20 billion below the figure that had been in the market, closer to $50 billion than to $70 billion. The companies that sell the chips and the data-center build fell first. The Philadelphia semiconductor index lost 3.39%. Micron fell 4.79%, Broadcom 4.35%, Oracle 5.48% and Nvidia 2.94%. The Nasdaq lost 1.25%, to 27,193.34. The Dow rose 0.10% and the Russell was flat. The damage was narrow, which is why the week could still finish green.

Friday took most of the index back. The S&P rose 0.6% to 7,811.54, the Dow 0.8% to 51,654.95 and the Nasdaq 0.6% to 27,366.17. For the week that is the S&P up 1.2%, the Dow up 0.9% and the Nasdaq up 0.6%, with the Russell down 0.9% at 2,806.98. Year to date the S&P is up 14.1%, the Nasdaq 17.7%, the Dow 7.5% and the Russell 13.1%. The VIX finished at 14.84, down from 15.31. A bad Thursday for chips did not become a week in which insurance on the index got more expensive.

The largest companies split along the AI supply chain. Amazon rose 4.3% to $262.43 and Microsoft 3.4% to $535.07. Nvidia fell 2.0% to $229.28, the worst of the eight biggest, after that Thursday drop. Broadcom still finished the week up 1.8% to $361.54, because the other four sessions outweighed Thursday. Alphabet rose 2.4% to $351.66, Tesla 3.3% to $382.70 and Apple 0.9% to $336.64. Meta fell 1.3% to $718.67.

Sectors tell the same story with the stock-picking taken out. Utilities rose 3.97%, consumer staples 3.60% and energy 3.60%. Health care rose 2.79%, consumer discretionary 2.55%, financials 2.32%, real estate 1.96% and materials 1.17%. Communication services was flat, up 0.05%, and only because Friday's carrier crash erased the rest of its week. Industrials fell 0.41%. Technology fell 0.52%, last place, about 4.5 percentage points behind utilities. The S&P rose in a week its technology sector fell. That is the sentence to remember.

TL;DR The S&P and the Nasdaq set records on Tuesday with the 10-year above 5%. Thursday's OpenAI report knocked semiconductors 3.4%, and Friday repaired the indexes more than the chip stocks. Defensive sectors led. Technology finished last.
Telecom

SpaceX bought spectrum, and the carriers were repriced in an afternoon

Friday's other story had nothing to do with the 10-year. SpaceX said it agreed to buy a nationwide portfolio of low-band spectrum, a step toward making Starlink Mobile a national wireless carrier. The three companies that already own that business were the three worst stocks in the S&P 500 that day. T-Mobile fell 13.27%, AT&T 10.82% and Verizon 10.14%.

The tower companies moved the other way, and the reason is the business model. Crown Castle rose 15.60%, American Tower 9.30% and SBA Communications 7.34%. Carriers rent space on those towers. A fourth national network is a new tenant if you own the steel, and a new competitor if you own the subscribers. Communication services, the sector that holds the three carriers, finished the week flat because of those three Friday moves. One afternoon rewrote a sector that had been having a fine week.

Humana rose 11.56% the same day after better Medicare Advantage star ratings for 2027. Two different policy headlines, two double-digit moves, on a session the S&P rose 0.6%. Friday was a day the index was the least interesting number on the page.

TL;DR A SpaceX spectrum deal cut the three big carriers 10% to 13% on Friday and lifted the tower owners that would rent to a new entrant. The sector's flat week is those three stocks.
Energy & consumer

Oil followed the headlines again, and Pepsi showed what a low bar looks like

Brent did another round trip on diplomacy. The December contract jumped 4.07% on Thursday, on reports the United States might resume large-scale operations in Iran and on worry that a storm would hit offshore production. Friday it eased after President Trump announced a deal with Vladimir Putin for Russia to supply diesel, and it settled at $104.72, still up 2.4% on the week. WTI's November contract rose 0.8% to $91.85. The gap between them, about $13 a barrel, is still the Hormuz premium. Brent prices the seaborne barrel that has to deal with the strait. WTI prices U.S. crude that does not. Energy stocks rose 3.60% with Brent, a reversal from late September, when refiners fell on export-ban talk while crude went nowhere.

PepsiCo reported Thursday morning, the first big consumer company of the season. Revenue rose 5.6% to $25.27 billion, ahead of the $24.96 billion analysts expected. Organic revenue, which strips out currency and deals, rose 3.1%. Core earnings were $2.34 a share against a $2.29 consensus. Management then cut the year's core earnings-growth forecast to 2.5–3.5%, from a previous range of 5–7%. The stock rose 3.7% anyway.

The lesson is the bar. A guidance cut hurts when investors were still counting on the old number. When the stock has already fallen far enough that the old number was not in the price, the quarter's beat is the new information. North America is still the soft spot. International organic revenue grew 8%, the part of the report that did the work. Staples as a sector rose 3.60% on the week, in the same tape where lower-income consumers are the group feeling inflation first. The defensive bid and the Pepsi beat are the same story told two ways.

TL;DR Brent finished up 2.4% at $104.72 after a Thursday spike on Iran headlines and a Friday fade on a Russia diesel deal. Pepsi beat, cut its earnings outlook, and the stock rose, because expectations had already fallen.

The AI trade

Landlords up, shovel-sellers down

One revenue report split the trade that had been moving together. The companies that rent out computing rose. The companies that sell the chips, and the one financing a huge build, fell.

01

The revenue number

The Financial Times reported, from documents shared with investors, that OpenAI's annualized revenue was about $20 billion below the $70 billion figure that had been circulating, closer to $50 billion. That is a demand question. If the customer at the center of the buildout is smaller than the market had been told, the companies selling it chips and data centers get repriced in an afternoon. On Thursday, they did. The semiconductor index fell 3.39%.

02

Landlords versus shovel-sellers

Amazon and Microsoft rent computing power to AI companies and sell it on. They led the megacaps, up 4.3% and 3.4%, with most of that on Friday. Nvidia sells the chips those data centers fill, and it was the worst of the eight largest stocks, down 2.0% at $229.28. Oracle, which has been financing a large data-center build, fell 5.48% on Thursday. The market is starting to separate "we sell the capacity" from "we are spending to build the capacity."

03

The index hid it

Technology fell 0.5% in a week the S&P rose 1.2%. The Nasdaq still finished higher, because Amazon, Microsoft and the rest of the big weights offset a semiconductor index that dropped 3.39% in one session. If you own the S&P, you had a good week. If what you actually own inside it is the chips, you had Thursday.

Mechanics

Six things worth actually understanding

If you are new to the club, start here. Every one of these shows up in the stories above.

Markets

A record with the 10-year above 5%

S&P record 7,818.93  ·  10-year 5.27% that day  ·  last time: July 19, 2007

Stocks can rise while yields rise when earnings growth is doing the work. They struggle when the yield rise is about inflation that will not come down. Tuesday was that kind of day. Next Wednesday's CPI is the test of whether it stays that way.

A discount rate is the rate you use to turn next year's cash into today's price. The 10-year is the starting point for that rate. A higher 10-year makes the same earnings worth less today, unless the earnings grow enough to offset it.

Indexes

Why Caterpillar moved the Dow and barely touched the S&P

Dow −341 pts  ·  Caterpillar −5.75% to $813.83  ·  S&P −0.22%

The Dow adds up share prices. A stock at $800 moves it more than a stock at $200, even when the cheaper stock is the bigger company. The S&P adds up market value, so a giant company matters more than an expensive share.

That is why one industrial name can drag the Dow on a day the S&P barely moves. If the two indexes disagree, check which one is being pushed by a high share price before you call it a broad selloff.

Bonds

The close versus the spike

Monday close 5.31%  ·  Wednesday intraday 5.36%  ·  Friday close 5.24%

Monday's close, 5.31% on the Treasury par curve, is the number in the headlines. Wednesday's 5.36% was a trade during the day. The par curve closed that day at 5.28%.

When someone says the 10-year "hit" a number, ask whether they mean a trade during the session or the close the Treasury publishes that afternoon. This letter's table uses the close. The par curve is the yield on a bond priced at 100, interpolated across maturities. It is the clean series to compare week to week.

Bonds

Duration: why a 1 point move in yields is an 8% move in price

price change ≈ − duration × change in yield

Duration measures how many years of cash flows you are waiting for, and it tells you how hard the price swings. A bond with a duration of about 8 falls about 8% if its yield rises 1 percentage point. The sign is negative because price and yield move in opposite directions.

Thursday's meeting walked through this before the pitch drill. The 10-year near 5.3% is the rate inside that sum for mortgages, for corporate bonds, and for the discount rate on a stock. A stock is a claim on cash that arrives over many years, so it has a duration too. That is why a higher 10-year presses the multiple even when the company's earnings have not changed.

Companies

Why towers rose when carriers fell

carriers −10% to −13%  ·  towers +7% to +16%  ·  same headline

A new mobile network competes with T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon for subscribers. It rents the towers from Crown Castle, American Tower and SBA. One headline, two businesses.

Before you trade a headline, write down who loses the customer and who collects the rent. The same test works on the AI trade this week. Nvidia sells the chip. Amazon and Microsoft rent out the computer that chip sits in.

Earnings

A beat, a cut, and a stock that rose

$2.34 vs $2.29  ·  guidance 5–7% cut to 2.5–3.5%  ·  stock +3.7%

Pepsi's core earnings beat by about five cents, and the company cut the year's earnings-growth range roughly in half. The stock rose. The market trades the gap between the news and what was already in the price.

If everyone already expected the cut, the cut is not new information. The beat is. That is why a worse outlook can come with a higher share price, and why "they beat" on its own never tells you which way the stock should go.

TL;DR Ask three questions of any big move this week. Was it the close or a spike during the day? Who loses the customer, and who collects the rent? And was the news actually worse than the price already assumed?

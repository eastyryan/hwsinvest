Rates

The Fed hiked, unanimously, and the projections did the talking

The FOMC raised the target range to 3.75–4.00% on Wednesday, the first increase since 2023. The vote was 12 to 0. That number is the story. In July the same committee voted 9 to 3, with three officials dissenting in favour of a hike, and last issue we said to read the vote before the headline. The split closed completely.

Kevin Warsh was blunt about why. "The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long," he said, and on the standard he had set at Jackson Hole: "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Today, the FOMC decided that this standard has not been satisfied." The action, he said, "will support a timelier return to the committee's 2 percent goal."

The dot plot is where the real information was. The median official now puts the rate at 4.00–4.25% at the end of 2026, which implies one more hike before the year is out, and at 4.00–4.25% again at the end of 2027. Sixteen officials pencilled in at least one further increase this year and four pencilled in two. Only four projected any cuts in 2027.

Put that against June, when the same median showed 3.75–4.00% for 2026 and 3.50–3.75% for 2027. In three months the committee has moved from projecting half a point of cuts to projecting half a point of hikes. The accompanying forecasts are equally frank: PCE inflation at 3.7% this year and 2.3% next, and no return to the 2% target until 2029.

One scorecard worth keeping. Last issue laid out three landing spots for the end-2027 median: 3.75–4.00% for dovish, 4.25–4.50% for in line, and 4.75% or higher for hawkish. It came in at 4.00–4.25%, between dovish and in line. Hawkish against June, gentler than the market had feared. That is exactly why the long end did not sell off.

TL;DR The Fed hiked a quarter point to 3.75–4.00% by a unanimous 12 to 0, after a 9 to 3 split in July. The median dot now shows one more hike this year and rates near 4.00–4.25% through the end of 2027, against June projections that showed cuts. The Fed does not expect 2% inflation until 2029.
Rates

The front end sold off, the long end did not, and that is the point

The curve did something specific this week. The 2-year added 13 bp to 4.76%, a 52-week high. The 10-year added 5 bp to 5.01%. The 30-year fell 1 bp to 5.34%. The 2s10s spread is now 25 bp, the flattest reading in this series.

On Wednesday afternoon it was starker still: short-dated yields rose while the 10-year fell about 4 bp and the 30-year about 5 bp. A central bank raising rates and the long end rallying at the same time is not a contradiction. It is the bond market saying it believes the Fed will get inflation down, so the compensation it needs for lending thirty years does not have to rise.

Thursday extended it. The 10-year touched 4.94% on the par curve and, as James DePorre put it, "The question all week was whether yields would break decisively through 5% or stabilize, and on Thursday they stabilized, which let the chips and the big-cap technology names catch a bid." Friday gave it back and the 10-year closed the week at 5.01%.

The supply test comes now. Treasury sells $78bn of 2-year notes on Tuesday, $70bn of 5-years on Wednesday and $44bn of 7-years on Thursday. That is $192bn in three days, into a front end that just repriced. Demand at those auctions is the cleanest read available on whether anyone actually wants this paper at these yields.

TL;DR The 2-year rose 13 basis points to a 52-week high while the 30-year fell one, flattening 2s10s to 25 basis points. A long end that rallies while the Fed hikes is a market that trusts the inflation fight. Next week brings $192bn of 2, 5 and 7-year supply as the test.
Equities

A flat index hiding a real rotation

The S&P 500 finished the week at 7,650.50, down 0.08%. That number tells you almost nothing. Underneath it the Nasdaq gained 0.7%, the Dow lost 1.7% for a third straight losing week, and the Russell 2000 lost 0.9%. On Friday technology rose 0.87% and industrials 0.47%, and every other sector fell.

The VIX is the cleanest evidence that this was an event week. It closed at 14.81, down 6.5% on the week and down 16.4% from Wednesday's close. Uncertainty gets priced before a known event and released after it, regardless of the outcome. Worth noting alongside it: three-month volatility sits at 18.24, a 23% premium to the 30-day reading. Calm now, turbulence expected later.

The consumer data landed the same morning as the Fed and pointed the same way. August retail sales rose 1.2% against a 0.7% forecast, with the control group up 1.4%. Heather Long of Navy Federal Credit Union: "American consumers are still opening their wallets and buying. August retail sales showed a big bounce back after a weak July." Oxford Economics' Michael Pearce cautioned that some of it was "seasonal noise" from Amazon Prime Day timing.

TL;DR The S&P finished flat for the week while the Nasdaq rose 0.7% and the Dow fell 1.7%, so money rotated rather than left. The VIX fell to 14.81, down 16.4% from Wednesday, because the Fed event passed. Retail sales rose 1.2% against a 0.7% forecast, which made the hike easier to justify.
Geopolitics

Trump escalated all weekend, and oil fell four percent

The rhetoric hardened sharply. Trump told Axios on September 17: "I have a big decision coming up. Do I want to go in and annihilate them, or do I not?" He returned early from Camp David on Saturday evening, and by Sunday was saying he was in "deciding mode," that "very big things" could happen soon, and that Tehran had better behave.

The facts on the water hardened too. On Friday the Revolutionary Guards claimed they struck a tanker, the Trend, inside the Strait of Hormuz, and UK Maritime Trade Operations confirmed incidents in the waterway. By Sunday US Central Command had redirected 109 commercial vessels near the strait to enforce the blockade of Iranian ports.

Then futures opened Sunday evening and did the opposite of what the headlines suggest. Crude fell about 4.3% to roughly $96. S&P 500 futures rose 0.95%. Nasdaq 100 futures rose 1.33% and traded through 30,000. Gold slipped.

The reason is on the calendar rather than in the quotes. On Tuesday Trump meets the leaders of all six Gulf states at the UN General Assembly, and Iran has already passed seven conditions for restarting talks through Qatar and Pakistan: end the fighting on all fronts, unfreeze its funds, lift the naval blockade, and stop the attacks on Hezbollah. Iranian security chief Mohsen Rezaei confirmed on September 19 that those consultations were live. Add Friday's news that Aramco is restarting the pipeline, and the market is looking at a scheduled negotiation with a published agenda.

This is worth sitting with, because it is the single most common mistake in reading news. A price does not move on how alarming a statement sounds. It moves on the change in the expected outcome. Loud threats immediately before a scheduled meeting look to a trader like negotiating posture. A meeting on the calendar with all six Gulf states at the table looks like a path to fewer barrels being blocked.

TL;DR Trump spent the weekend threatening to annihilate the Iranian regime, the Revolutionary Guards struck a tanker in Hormuz and CENTCOM redirected 109 vessels. Crude still fell 4.3% to about $96 on Sunday night while equity futures rose, because Trump meets six Gulf leaders at the UN on Tuesday and Iran has already sent conditions through Qatar.
Energy

Oil gave back its panic premium

Last issue flagged that the Saudi buffer would run out mid-week. It did, and then the situation improved rather than breaking. Saudi Aramco now expects to partially restart the East-West pipeline within days and to reach full capacity within about six weeks. The line normally carries up to 7m barrels a day and is the route that bypasses the Strait of Hormuz entirely.

Prices eased on the news. Brent settled Friday at $103.87 and WTI at $100.30, both down modestly on the week. Scott Shelton of TP ICAP: "It feels like the physical panic is over for now in crude with the partial resumption of the Yanbu route out of Saudi Arabia."

Two things keep this from being a clean all-clear. Aramco told European refining customers they will receive no crude allocations next month, which is what a company does when it is short of barrels rather than long of them. And a tanker was struck by an unknown projectile near the strait on Wednesday. Priyanka Sachdeva of Phillip Nova called the move "a cooling of the geopolitical risk premium rather than a fundamental reversal."

TL;DR Aramco expects a partial restart of the East-West pipeline within days and full capacity within six weeks, and Brent eased to $103.87 with WTI at $100.30. But European refiners were told they get no allocations next month and a tanker was hit near the strait on Wednesday, so this is a cooling risk premium rather than a fix.
Markets

Buffett steps down at 96, and the succession is finally complete

Warren Buffett stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway on Friday, ending more than fifty years in the role. He is 96. His son Howard Buffett, a director since 1993, becomes chairman. Warren becomes chairman emeritus and stays on the board. "Father Time always wins," he said.

Greg Abel remains chief executive, the job he took when Buffett stepped down from it in late 2025. So the split is now explicit: Abel runs the business and allocates the capital, Howard holds the chair. Abel's comment was that "Warren's impact on Berkshire and its owners is without parallel in the history of American business," and framed Howard's role as guarding the culture rather than picking the investments.

That division is the thing to understand. Howard Buffett is not being handed his father's job. The chairmanship at Berkshire was deliberately designed as a cultural anchor, a non-executive role whose purpose is to preserve the way the company behaves if a future board is ever tempted to behave differently. The capital allocation, which is what actually produced the record, sits with Abel.

For a club that studies investors, this is the end of the single longest track record in the business, and it is worth saying that it ends by handover rather than by disaster. Most great investing careers do not.

TL;DR Buffett stepped down as Berkshire chairman on Friday at 96 after more than fifty years, with son Howard taking the chair and Warren becoming chairman emeritus. Greg Abel stays CEO and keeps capital allocation. The chairmanship is a cultural role, not the investing job.

The AI trade

AI and the big equities

AI policy

The biggest supplier in AI says there will be no slowdown

Last issue covered three lab chief executives asking the industry to pace itself. This week the company that sells them all their hardware answered. At Dreamforce on Tuesday, Nvidia's Jensen Huang said AI developers should "run as fast as you can," and elsewhere that "we are not going to have a slowdown."

His alternative to coordinated restraint was simple and puts the responsibility on each company: rather than asking for government intervention, wait to release a product until you know it is safe. That is a meaningfully different proposal from Amodei's, which asks for independent evaluators embedded inside labs and eventual international limits.

Follow the incentives before you decide who is right. Nvidia's revenue is a direct function of how fast frontier labs train. A slower frontier is a smaller order book, and it arrives later. That does not make Huang wrong, but it does mean his position is the one you would predict from his position on the value chain, exactly as Amodei's is from his.

The market sided with Huang this week, at least for a few days. Chips and large-cap technology rallied on Thursday once the 10-year stabilised below 5%, and the Nasdaq was the only major index to finish the week higher.

TL;DR Jensen Huang rejected the slowdown at Dreamforce on Tuesday, saying companies should run as fast as they can and should self-police by not shipping until a product is safe. Nvidia's revenue depends directly on the pace of frontier training. Chips and big-cap tech rallied Thursday and the Nasdaq was the only index up on the week.
AI policy

The pledge is now a federal antitrust case

On Friday four paying subscribers of ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and Gemini filed suit against Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI and Google in the US District Court for the Northern District of California. The case is Buist v. Anthropic PBC. The claim is a Sherman Act violation: that an agreement among competitors to restrict the pace of product development is illegal coordination, whatever its stated motive.

The plaintiffs' timeline is the heart of it. Amodei published his essay on September 12 and Altman, Musk and Demis Hassabis endorsed it the same day. The suit reads same-day agreement among four rivals as coordination rather than coincidence. They want class certification for subscribers nationwide and an injunction blocking the arrangement. Lead attorney Nick Rowley: "AI safety should not be controlled by private agreements among the world's most powerful for-profit technology companies."

Note who is making this argument now. Two weeks ago the objection came from the White House, where David Sacks called the proposal regulatory capture. Now it comes from private plaintiffs calling it a cartel. Those are different legal theories arriving at the same place, and together they are a real obstacle.

The equity implication runs opposite to intuition. The bear case for AI infrastructure was that a genuine slowdown pushes compute demand further into the future. If courts or regulators break the pledge apart, that bear case weakens and the build-out timetable stays where it was.

TL;DR Four subscribers sued Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI and Google on September 18 in the Northern District of California, claiming the slowdown pledge is illegal coordination under the Sherman Act, and are seeking an injunction. The White House had already called it regulatory capture. If the pledge is broken up, the AI capex bear case weakens.
